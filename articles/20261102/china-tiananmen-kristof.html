在天安門屠殺現場，我目睹了中國人民非凡的勇氣

I Covered the Tiananmen Square Massacre. It Still Weighs on Me.

紀思道 • 2026-11-02
This story is part of a series marking 175 years of eyewitness journalism from The New York Times. Read more here.
本文是《紐約時報》為紀念創刊175週年來堅守現場報導而推出的系列報導之一。閱讀更多內容。
As I pedaled my bicycle toward the gunfire and Tiananmen Square, dodging people in crowds fleeing in the opposite direction, I kept thinking: What a crazy occupation this is.
當我騎著單車朝槍聲傳來的方向、向天安門廣場趕去時，一路躲避著迎面逃散的人群，心裡不停地想：這真是個瘋狂的職業。
For weeks during the spring of 1989, the question preoccupying the world had been whether the student-led Tiananmen demonstrations paralyzing China would end with a democratic revolution or with massacres. That night, June 3, the phone began ringing in the apartment in Beijing I shared with my wife and fellow New York Times correspondent, Sheryl WuDunn. We had our answer: Tens of thousands of army troops were forcing their way into central Beijing, mowing down crowds of unarmed protesters and bystanders who tried to resist.
1989年春天的那幾週，全世界都在關注一個問題：由學生領導、使中國陷入癱瘓的天安門示威究竟會以一場民主革命告終，還是以大屠殺收場？6月3日那天夜裡，我和妻子、同為《紐約時報》記者的伍潔芳(Sheryl WuDunn)在北京的公寓裡，電話開始響個不停。我們得到了答案：數萬名軍人正強行向北京市中心推進，向試圖阻擋他們的手無寸鐵的抗議者和旁觀者開槍射擊。
So I jumped on my bicycle, legs pumping madly, careening around tank traps students had set up, so I could report on a modern army waging war on its people.
於是我跳上單車，雙腿拚命蹬著，繞過學生們設置的坦克障礙，趕去報導一支現代化軍隊如何向本國人民開戰。 
It was probably the most important story I’ve covered and I got at least one big thing wrong — I’ll get to that — but I did manage to reach Tiananmen Square before the troops. I parked my bicycle near the famous portrait of Chairman Mao and began interviewing people in the throng of students and workers.
這也許是我記者生涯中報導過的最重要的新聞，而我至少犯了一個重大錯誤——這一點我稍後會談到。不過，我確實趕在軍隊抵達之前進入了天安門廣場。我把單車停在那幅著名的毛主席畫像附近，開始採訪聚集在廣場上的學生和工人。
Although it was already dark, the square was lit by overhead lights and by the glow of two armored personnel carriers that protesters had set ablaze. At first, the troops, arriving by the truckload, fired over our heads. Then they lowered their aim and fired right at us.
雖然天已經黑了，廣場仍被高處的燈光和兩輛被抗議者點燃的裝甲運兵車發出的火光照亮。起初，乘坐一輛輛卡車抵達的軍人朝我們頭頂上方開槍。隨後，他們壓低槍口，直接向我們射擊。

The gunfire was episodic, coming in cacophonous bursts every few minutes. People would fall, we would retreat, the guns would fall silent for a time and our retreat would halt.
槍聲時斷時續，每隔幾分鐘便是一陣刺耳的密集槍聲。有人倒下，我們後撤，槍聲沉寂下來，我們也停下後撤的腳步。 
I was rushing around, interviewing people in Chinese, always trying to keep a layer of protesters between me and the bullets. Until I realized that I was taller than almost everyone around me, so that a valuable few upper inches of me were still exposed. I began stooping as I spoke to people.
我四處奔走，用中文採訪，同時盡量藉助人群掩護自己，躲避子彈。直到後來我才意識到，自己比周圍幾乎所有人都高，這意味著，我仍有要命的一小截身體暴露在槍口之下。於是，我開始彎著腰與人交談。 
“General strike!” the crowd shouted. “General strike!” Some people hurled rocks or Molotov cocktails toward the troops, but they couldn’t hit the soldiers — and then there would be another round of gunfire, and more would drop.
「大罷工！大罷工！」人群高喊著。有人朝軍隊投擲石塊或燃燒瓶，但打不到他們。緊接著又是一輪槍響，更多人應聲倒下。
Groups of protesters commandeered two buses in hopes of reaching the troops. About a dozen young people boarded each bus, holding iron bars or other weapons with which they intended to attack the soldiers. The crowd screamed encouragement, but their courage was misplaced. As the protesters drove the buses toward the troops, the soldiers opened fire. The buses came to a halt, one of them on fire, and in a moment, these young people transformed from heroes into corpses.
有幾群抗議者強佔了兩輛公車，希望藉此衝到軍隊跟前。每輛車上約有十幾個年輕人，手裡拿著鐵棍或其他武器，打算攻擊士兵。人群高聲為他們鼓勁，但這份勇氣用錯了地方。當抗議者駕著公車駛向軍隊時，士兵開火了。兩輛車停了下來，其中一輛起了火，轉眼之間，這些年輕人就從英雄變成了屍體。
I found myself in the midst of a massacre. With each round of gunfire, we were pushed backward on the blood-soaked Avenue of Eternal Peace. My bicycle was now close to the troops and not recoverable, so I ran to a nearby hospital to count corpses, and then back home. I had forgotten that it was a Saturday evening, which for the paper meant early deadlines.
我發現自己身處一場屠殺之中。每一輪槍聲響起，都迫使我們沿著血跡斑斑的長安街向後退。我的單車此時已離軍隊太近，無法取回，於是我跑到附近一家醫院清點屍體，隨後又趕回家中。我忘了那天是星期六晚上，而對報社來說，這意味著截稿時間要提前。
When I didn’t return on time, Sheryl, who had filed her own story about the slaughter, was increasingly fearful that I had been shot. “There’s no point in worrying,” the foreign editor, Joseph Lelyveld, kept telling her on the phone; the anxiety in his voice made her worry even more. He tried to distract her by asking her to reach out to all the hospitals to document the death toll — a request that didn’t help Sheryl’s mood.
我遲遲沒有回來，已經發出自己那篇屠殺報導的伍潔芳越來越擔心我中了槍。「擔心也沒用，」國際新聞編輯約瑟夫·萊利維爾德在電話裡不斷對她說，可他聲音裡的焦慮反而讓她更加擔憂。他想分散她的注意力，請她聯繫所有醫院，統計死亡人數，這個要求絲毫沒有緩解她的不安。
Finally, I burst in, hugged Sheryl, called New York to reassure editors there that I was fine and then filed my story by telex — a few paragraphs at a time, as I wrote them. I had never been so sad to have my byline and Sheryl’s under a banner headline across the front page.
後來我終於衝進家門，擁抱伍潔芳，給紐約打電話，向編輯們報平安，然後用電傳發出了我的稿子，寫一段，發一段。看到我和伍潔芳的名字並列署在頭版通欄大標題下，我從未像那一刻那樣悲傷。
Guilt nagged at me that night, and this brings me to my first great mistake in covering the crisis. During the demonstrations in the weeks before the bloodshed, I had periodically written condescendingly that the workers and peasants in the protest movement talked about democracy but couldn’t define it. I had suggested that they were driven by anger at inflation and corruption but didn’t necessarily believe in democracy for Tibetans or fully embrace the kind of free and pluralistic society they spoke of so glowingly.
那天夜裡，愧疚一直折磨著我。這就涉及我報導這場危機時犯下的第一個重大錯誤。在流血事件發生前的幾個星期裡，在報導示威活動時，我不時以居高臨下的口吻寫道，參加抗議的工人和農民雖然談論民主，卻說不清民主究竟是什麼。我曾暗示，他們參加抗議是出於對通貨膨脹和腐敗的不滿，卻未必相信藏人也應享有民主權利，也未必完全認同他們熱情讚頌的那種自由、多元的社會。
There was something to this idea, but that night in June I had seen extraordinary courage from the workers and peasants. I was terrified that evening — my notebook was so damp with sweat from fear that I had trouble writing in it — and the bravest people of all were the poorly educated laborers from the countryside who drove bicycle rickshaws. Whenever there was a pause in the shooting, they would pedal their carts into the fray to pick up the bodies of students who had been killed or injured, then turn around and race to the hospitals. They might not have been able to define democracy but they were ready to die for it.
這種看法並非毫無道理。但6月的那個夜晚，我親眼目睹了工人和農民非凡的勇氣。那天晚上我害怕極了，筆記本被我因恐懼而流出的汗水浸得濕透，幾乎在上面寫不了字。而所有人中最勇敢的是那些來自農村、受教育程度不高、靠蹬三輪車謀生的勞動者。每當槍聲稍歇，他們便蹬著三輪車衝進交火地帶，把中彈身亡或受傷的學生抬上車，再掉頭飛奔醫院。他們或許說不清民主的定義，卻願意為民主獻出生命。
Another hard lesson I learned covering Tiananmen: Victims lie or, more charitably, misremember. It’s human nature to doubt oppressors and believe victims, but in the aftermath of the massacre there were many “eyewitness” accounts claiming that thousands or tens of thousands of students had been slaughtered in Tiananmen Square; that huge numbers of them had been shot from machine gun emplacements there; or that many were killed when tanks rolled over tents with students inside. None of this was true.
報導天安門事件時，我還學到了另一個沉痛的教訓：受害者也會說謊。或者更寬容地說，他們也會記錯事實。懷疑壓迫者、相信受害者，這是人之常情，但在屠殺發生後，有許多「目擊者」聲稱，有數千甚至數萬名學生在天安門廣場遭到屠殺，其中大量學生被廣場上架設的機槍射殺，還有許多學生在坦克碾過他們棲身的帳篷時喪生。這些說法都不屬實。

Since then, I’ve seen many times in my career that perpetrators of atrocities lie to cover up their abuses, and that victims respond with exaggerations of their own. This has taught me that we must be as vigilant in verifying accounts of victims as we are in grilling oppressors. While many estimates of the Tiananmen death toll are in the thousands, I’m reasonably sure these are wrong. Sheryl and I made great efforts to cultivate sources at hospitals and morgues to determine the toll, and our best guess is that between 400 and 800 people were killed and several thousand injured.
此後，在我的記者生涯中，我多次看到暴行的實施者為了掩蓋罪行而撒謊，受害者則以自己的誇大之詞作出回應。這讓我明白，我們核實受害者證詞時，必須像質詢壓迫者一樣保持警惕。雖然有許多估計認為天安門事件造成數千人死亡，但我相當確信，這些數字是錯誤的。我和伍潔芳曾竭盡全力在醫院和停屍房發展信源，以確定死亡人數。我們最合理的估計是，死亡人數在400至800人之間，另有數千人受傷。
Accessing hospitals was difficult. Soldiers turned us away at entrances and doctors were threatened if they spoke to journalists. Chinese citizens and even some officials cooperated nonetheless, because they wanted the truth to come out. Disenchanted employees at one hospital sneaked us inside through a tunnel system from a neighboring building. We found that even the hallways were filled with shooting victims.
進入醫院並不容易。士兵在入口處阻攔我們，醫生如果與記者交談也會受到威脅。儘管如此，仍有中國公民願意配合，甚至一些官員也是如此，因為他們希望真相能夠公之於眾。一家醫院裡，有幾名心灰意冷的員工悄悄帶我們從鄰近建築的地下通道進入醫院。我們發現，就連走廊裡也躺滿了槍傷患者。
One young wife was weeping beside the unconscious figure of her husband, an inventor with seven patents in his name. Despite her warnings, he had gone out when the shooting started, in order to support the students, and she loved and admired him for his courage. But, she added between sobs, she hated that he might leave her alone with their 4-year-old daughter.
一位年輕妻子在昏迷不醒的丈夫身旁哭泣。她的丈夫是一名發明家，名下擁有七項專利。槍擊開始後，儘管妻子曾勸阻，他還是出門聲援學生。她愛他，也欽佩他的勇氣。但她抽泣著補充說，她恨他可能就這樣丟下自己和他們四歲的女兒。
Journalists like to project toughness, but I was shattered, physically and morally exhausted. We went days with barely a wink of sleep, and once, when we did nap, the army fired on our apartment compound with automatic weapons. Moving around Beijing to interview people, Sheryl and I were afraid of being shot, but worse was to see the hopeful, vibrant China we had been covering morph into a crushing dictatorship. I remember collapsing on my bed one day and finding tears trickling down my cheeks.
記者總喜歡錶現得堅強，但我當時已經徹底崩潰，身心俱疲。我們連續幾天幾乎沒有合眼。有一次，我們好不容易小睡片刻，軍隊卻用自動武器向我們居住的公寓區開火。我和伍潔芳在北京四處採訪時，一直擔心自己會中槍。但更令人痛苦的是，看著我們一直報導的那個充滿希望、生機勃勃的中國正在變成一個令人窒息的獨裁國家。我記得有一天，我癱倒在床上，眼淚順著臉頰流下來。
The Chinese government rewrote history, calling the protesters “hoodlums” who had engaged in “rioting.” The state media singled me out, denouncing me for spreading lies and “writing not news reports but fiction.” The next day I ordered lunch at an empty Sichuan restaurant, where the staff gathered in a corner, pointing to me and whispering. I feared they had recognized me and I wondered if they might poison my food. Then several waitresses approached my table. “Are you the one they mentioned on television last night?” one asked. “The reporter who lies?”
中國政府改寫了歷史，將抗議者稱為參與「暴亂」的「暴徒」。官方媒體專門點名譴責我，說我散布謊言，「寫的不是新聞報導，而是小說」。第二天，我在一家空蕩蕩的川菜館點了午飯，服務員們聚在角落裡，指著我竊竊私語。我擔心他們認出了我，不禁懷疑他們會不會在飯菜裡下毒。隨後，幾名女服務員走到我的餐桌前。「你就是昨天晚上電視裡說的那個人嗎？」其中一人問，「那個撒謊的記者？」
“Yes,” I said, and I started to explain, but she cut me off. “Never mind,” she said, beaming. “There’s no need to pay your bill. We’ll take care of it. We’re all ‘hoodlums’ here.”
「是的，」我回答，正要解釋，她卻打斷了我。「沒關係，」她笑容滿面地說，「不用付賬了，我們請客。我們這裡都是『暴徒』。」
As the government began mass arrests, one of our sources was a Chinese student whom we had inadvertently gotten into trouble. “Shawn,” as he called himself in English, was a student at Tsinghua University who had helped us cover the protests. Now he was being interrogated about his connection to us, yet he still dared to meet secretly with Sheryl — who is Chinese American and could melt into crowds in a way I could not — to update her on the crackdown at the universities. His courage was breathtaking, and we were frightened for him when he suddenly disappeared.
政府開始大規模逮捕，而我們的一名消息來源——一位中國學生——因我們的無心之失而陷入了麻煩。他給自己取了個英文名字叫「Shawn」（肖恩），是清華大學的學生，曾幫助我們報導抗議活動。如今，他正因為與我們的關係而受到審問。儘管如此，他仍敢冒險與伍潔芳祕密見面，向她通報當局在大學校園裡的鎮壓行動——伍潔芳是華裔美國人，能夠像普通中國人一樣融入人群，而我做不到。肖恩的勇氣令人驚嘆。當他突然失蹤時，我們非常擔心他的安危。

This incident brings me to another moment that I still turn over in my mind. More than a year after his disappearance, Shawn reappeared in Beijing, met with me in secret and explained what had happened: He had been imprisoned but had escaped and was on the run. Now he asked for my help in fleeing China.
這件事讓我想起另一個至今仍在腦海中反覆思量的時刻。失蹤一年多後，肖恩重新出現在北京，祕密與我見面，講述了他的遭遇：他曾被監禁，但後來越獄逃了出來，如今正在逃亡。他請求我幫助他逃離中國。
One basic rule of being a foreign correspondent is that you obey local laws. You certainly don’t help an escaped felon leave the country. Yet here was a young man who had endured prison because he had helped us cover this story. If we didn’t assist him, he would almost certainly be caught.
作為外派記者，有一條基本準則就是遵守所在國法律。無論如何，你都不應該幫助一名越獄的重罪犯逃離該國。然而，站在我面前的這個年輕人，正是因為幫助我們報導這件事才遭受牢獄之災。如果我們不幫他，他幾乎肯定會再次被捕。
What to do? I wanted to consult editors in New York, but that was impossible because my phones were tapped. In any case, no editor could recommend helping an escaped prisoner.
該怎麼辦？我想徵詢紐約編輯的意見，但這不可能，因為我的電話受到監聽。無論如何，也不會有哪個編輯建議幫助一名越獄囚犯。
Another concern: I harbored a nagging doubt about Shawn’s story. He said he had escaped while working in the fields of a prison farm, but we had never heard of anyone escaping from a Chinese prison. I wondered, Could this be a setup? The Chinese prime minister at the time, Li Peng, disliked my coverage, and his office was looking for an excuse to expel me. Was Chinese state security hoping to entrap me so that the authorities could arrest and imprison me and close The Times’s bureau?
我還有另一個顧慮：我始終對肖恩的說法存有一絲疑慮。他說自己是在監獄農場乾農活時逃出來的，但我們從未聽說過有人能從中國的監獄越獄。我不禁懷疑：這會不會是個圈套？當時的中國總理李鵬不喜歡我的報導，他的辦公室正在尋找藉口將我驅逐出境。中國國家安全機關是不是想誘我上鉤，好讓當局逮捕並監禁我，同時關閉《紐約時報》北京分社？
I wavered, but Sheryl had a firmer compass. She doubted that Shawn would agree to entrap me, and she insisted that, given the risks he had taken, we owed him our assistance. So we did help him, including providing money to finance his escape.
我猶豫不決，但伍潔芳的判斷更加堅定。她不相信肖恩會同意設局陷害我，而且她堅持認為，鑒於他曾冒過那麼大的風險，我們有義務幫助他。於是，我們確實出手相助，包括出錢資助他逃亡。
Shawn was able to slip into Hong Kong on Christmas Day, 1990, when he figured there would be fewer British police officers along the border. He called me that night, and the next day I flew to Hong Kong, where, with the help of Human Rights Watch, I negotiated to get him a visa to the United States. Shawn is now a U.S. citizen.
1990年聖誕節那天，肖恩成功潛入香港。他認為那天邊境一帶的英國警察可能會少一些。當天晚上，他給我打了電話。第二天，我飛往香港，在人權觀察組織的協助下，通過交涉為他爭取到了美國簽證。如今，肖恩已成為美國公民。

Once I was out of China and could speak freely on the phone, I confessed to the paper’s top editors what I had done. To my surprise, they were congratulatory rather than scolding. A retired executive editor, A.M. Rosenthal, confided that three decades earlier he had helped sneak someone from Communist Poland to the West. And a Times correspondent, Sydney Schanberg, had tried desperately (though unsuccessfully) to doctor a fake passport for a Cambodian colleague, Dith Pran, to save him from the Khmer Rouge slaughter. Dith Pran eventually made it out on his own and worked as a much-loved colleague in The Times’s newsroom, a constant reminder that we have complex human obligations as well as professional ones.
等我離開中國、能在電話裡自由交談時，我就向報社高層編輯坦白了自己的所作所為。出乎意料的是，他們非但沒有責備我，反而向我表示祝賀。已經退休的執行總編輯A·M·羅森塔爾私下告訴我，30年前，他曾幫助一名身在共產主義波蘭的人祕密逃往西方。《紐約時報》記者雪梨·尚伯格也曾竭盡全力，試圖為柬埔寨同事迪斯·普蘭偽造護照，幫助他逃過紅色高棉的屠殺，雖然最終沒有成功。迪斯·普蘭後來憑自己的努力逃了出來，在《紐約時報》的新聞編輯部成為了一位深受愛戴的同事。這時刻提醒著我們：除了職業責任，我們還肩負著複雜的人道義務。
There’s nothing I’ve done in my career that was as unprofessional as helping Shawn. But there are also few things I’m prouder of.
在我的整個職業生涯中，沒有哪件事比幫助肖恩更不符合職業規範。但也很少有哪件事能讓我如此自豪。
